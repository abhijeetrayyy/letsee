# Interest-graph social networks and consumption trackers: how they retained, grew, and died

Research date: 2026-09-10. Method: ~80 web searches and page fetches (founder interviews, postmortems, help-center docs, growth teardowns, user threads). Several primary sources (Fast Company, Cultured, Variety, IndieWire, Deadline, Letterboxd Journal) returned 403/paywall; where that happened, facts are taken from secondary coverage or search excerpts and marked as such. Numbers are as reported by the cited source on the cited date.

## 0. Executive summary (ten sentences)

1. Every tracker that lasted began as a single-player diary with a public profile, and every one that died lost either the diary's value (Foursquare, BeReal) or the community layer (Last.fm, Douban) or the business that paid for the servers (TV Time).
2. The retention engine is not the feed; it is the accumulating personal artifact (diary, collection, wantlist, map, streak calendar) plus a small number of people who can see it.
3. Passive capture wins where it is possible (Last.fm scrobble, Airbuds widget, Strava GPS, Trakt/Plex sync); where it is not (film, books, restaurants), the log must be a two-tap act with an identity payoff (Douban's "want to watch / watched", Letterboxd's diary, Beli's pairwise rank).
4. Identity display (Letterboxd Four Favorites, MySpace Top 8, Airbuds "Space") is the cheapest, most durable reason to keep a profile current, and it doubles as the marketing format.
5. Annual and weekly recaps (Wrapped, Year in Sport, Letterboxd Year in Review, Airbuds Weekly Recap, Goodreads Reading Challenge) are the only "notifications" people actively want, because they are about the user, not about the app.
6. The social unit that is growing in 2024-2026 is the group chat and the invite list (Partiful, Discord servers, Close Friends), not the follower graph; 41% of social interactions and 70%+ of Gen Z's "meaningful" ones now happen in private channels.
7. Deliberate subtraction of social features (StoryGraph: no DMs, no comments on reviews; Beli: no DMs, friends-only reviews) is now a growth feature, because the loudest churn driver in tracker communities is harassment and review bombing (Goodreads, Douban).
8. People happily pay for stats about themselves, profile decoration, and sync convenience (Letterboxd Pro/Patron, StoryGraph Plus, Trakt VIP); they revolt when previously free social features are paywalled (Strava 2020) or when prices double without new value (Trakt 2025) - though the utility usually survives the revolt.
9. Conversion is thin: 4% of Letterboxd actives pay, 2% of Strava registered, ~0.4% of StoryGraph and Backloggd users - so per-user infrastructure cost is the existential constraint (TV Time's premium tier covered ~10% of its costs).
10. Streaks and leaderboards work (Duolingo: CURR +21%, DAU 4.5x) but only when the underlying action is intrinsically daily and the system forgives (streak freeze keeps streaks 4.5x longer); forced daily rituals with no accumulating artifact decay within months (BeReal: 9% of Android installs opening daily two months after peak).

## 1. Case studies

### 1.1 Letterboxd
**History and numbers.** Founded 2011 by Matthew Buchanan and Karl von Randow, owners of the Auckland web studio Cactuslab, as a part-time side project ("we just wanted a cool app"). A private version was shown at the Brooklyn Beta conference in October 2011; an invitation-only beta opened 24 April 2012; public launch with a freemium model on 8 February 2013. Six months after Brooklyn Beta they had 17,000 beta testers who had written 80,000 reviews, made 10,000 lists and logged 2M+ films; by public launch ~40,000 members had contributed 400,000+ reviews, 44,000 lists and ~8M films (1M films logged by 16 Feb 2012). The founders paid themselves nothing for years and subsidized the site by pulling staff off agency work; it was unprofitable until 2019. Members: 1.8M (2020) -> 3M (Jan 2021) -> 4.1M (end 2021) -> 10M (Sep 2023) -> 11.4M (end 2023) -> 12M (Feb 2024) -> 15M (Jun 2024) -> 17M (end 2024) -> 30M+ (Jul 2026), with ~10M new members between Jan 2025 and Jan 2026. 2025 activity: 898.5M films logged (+28%), 143.6M reviews (+49%), 672.5M ratings (+36%), 12.9M lists (+88%). Demographics: 18-24 is the largest cohort, then 25-35; 80% under 45. Sep 2023: Tiny Ltd bought 60% (reported ~US$50-60M valuation; NZ$83M in NZ coverage); founders kept 20% each and stayed CEO/CTO; 40+ staff.

**Growth mechanics, in order of importance.** (1) The invite-only year seeded the site with designers and film obsessives from the conference crowd - "engaged, lucid and passionate" - so the public arrived to a full room. (2) The pandemic: home viewing plus a need for a diary took them from 1.8M to 3M in twelve months, and the growth never reverted. (3) Gen Z and TikTok: "Four Favorites" - the four posters at the top of every profile - became a red-carpet interview format; the Letterboxd TikTok's Four Favorites videos have been watched 10M+ times and actors (Olivia Rodrigo, Jesse Eisenberg, Scarlett Johansson) now prepare answers in advance. The profile feature is the marketing unit: a celebrity's four favorites is content, and every viewer wants their own four. Time Out London on a film-lovers' dating event: "Every first date I've been on includes the question: 'What is your Letterboxd top four?'"; Young Hollywood calls Letterboxd "Gen Z's new online personality test". (4) Year in Review: personal headline stats plus a community round-up; official posts draw 4M+ views and "#LetterboxdWrapped" shares run from 50-film to 500-film tallies; search interest peaks every January. (5) Industry embedding: preview screenings with distributors, showtimes in six countries, HQ accounts for theaters/festivals (Sep 2020), a Video Store (Dec 2025, 23 countries, TVOD) with an "Unreleased Gems" shelf from which five films were picked up for wider release, and the "Letterboxd Generation" phrase used by Linklater, NBCU and Warner Bros executives.

**Retention and civility.** The diary is the core object; reviews are optional and short; likes and follows exist, but there are no DMs, no algorithmic feed and no streaks. Notifications are narrow (see section 5). The community policy is deliberately short - "be cool" - and moderators hide flagged content from public view rather than always deleting it; the policy was updated to explicitly reject white-nationalist content. Critic Scott Tobias: "the safest space for film discussion we've got." Critiques: Vulture's "Boxxeding" piece on publicized celebrity accounts; Kate Lindsay (Embedded) on how strangers' ratings now override a partner's recommendation ("immediately opens up Letterboxd on his phone"); the Top Four as performance rather than taste (Izzy Scott). **Monetization:** Pro US$19/yr (ad-free, all-time and annual stats, filter by your streaming services, activity-feed filters, list cloning; at 2013 launch it was Netflix filtering, IMDb/CSV import and the year in review); Patron US$49/yr (custom posters and backdrops on profile/lists/reviews, Patrons directory, early access to extra stats). ~4% of active users pay (2023). TV logging was announced Sep 2023, promised "later this year" in 2024 and again in 2026; still limited to what TMDB exposes.

### 1.2 Strava
Strava is the largest fitness social network: 195M+ registered, ~50M MAU (2025), 40M activities uploaded per week (2022), ARR $490M in 2025 (+32%), 90% from subscriptions at $79.99/yr or $11.99/mo, with only ~2% of registered users paying. Its loop is passive capture (GPS from a watch or phone) -> automatic public artifact (map, splits) -> one-tap social acknowledgement (kudos) -> automatic competition (segments: any stretch of road becomes a leaderboard you land on without opting in) -> groups (clubs). 2025 Year in Sport: 14B kudos (+20% YoY), activities with photos get 3.1x the kudos, ~1M clubs (new clubs nearly 4x in 2025), and people who log group activities are more likely to still be active 12 months later than solo-only users. Engagement is quoted at 2.23% interaction per post vs Facebook's 0.15%.

Year in Sport is the annual "emotional upgrade button": built to be screenshotted, dropped when people are reflective, and used to upsell paid analytics. In May 2020, Strava moved full segment leaderboards (beyond top 10; seeing friends), routes and matched runs behind the paywall for 55M+ users, saying it was not profitable and that free API access was leaking the data. The backlash was loud (DC Rainmaker, BikeRadar, the5krunner) but the GPS diary held people; subscriptions became the business. Notifications: kudos, comments, club posts (per-club: all / announcements only / none), segment and achievement emails, challenges; commenting or giving kudos on an activity subscribes you to its thread; Strava deliberately suppresses some notifications (e.g., losing a KOM on a downhill segment) "where doing so might be a poor experience".

### 1.3 Goodreads and The StoryGraph
**Goodreads** (2006; ~150M users; Amazon since 2013) is the category's cautionary tale for what happens when a tracker stops iterating: outdated design, weak moderation, and a social layer that "breeds division". Review bombing is structural - the 2023 Cait Corrain case (an author used sock-puppet accounts to one-star rivals and lost her book deal), extortion scams against self-published authors (TIME, 2021), pre-publication pile-ons (Elizabeth Gilbert's withdrawn novel), and, next, AI-written reviews from fake accounts. A 2023 pledge to strengthen account verification changed little. And yet the Reading Challenge is the stickiest annual ritual in the category: ~8M participants pledged 342.8M books in 2023 (avg 43 each); 9M+ pledged 356M+ in 2024. A pledge in January is a re-engagement contract for the year, and a progress bar that is always slightly behind.

**The StoryGraph** (Nadia Odunayo, London, bootstrapped, solo developer for 2.5 years) is the modern playbook for cold-starting against an incumbent. She spent three months interviewing readers before writing code, cold-DM'd book bloggers on Instagram for feedback, ran a landing page ("We're building a tool for avid readers") aimed at 52-books-a-year readers, posted her own reviews on Instagram before the product existed, and sent a weekly progress email to ~100 supporters ("it's like it's your weekly stand up"). Beta: 100 users (Jan 2020) -> 1,000 (Jun 2020) -> a viral tweet during the summer of 2020 (Black-woman-founded, Amazon-free alternative) brought tens of thousands in three days and took the site down for two weeks (email queue of 18-20k; Goodreads imports paused) -> 100k (Jan 2021) -> 2.3-2.4M registered (2024) -> 3.8M active (Feb 2025). MAU runs 25-33% of registered. Goodreads CSV import was "critical onboarding". Retention features from research: mood/pace filtering, stats (power users), buddy reads, reading challenges, book clubs. Deliberate subtraction: no DMs, no comments on reviews - "where anyone can just comment on your review and you've got to deal with being scared to put reviews up there" is exactly what she designed out. Monetization: Plus was pre-sold (1,400 pre-orders at $30-50 for a product that did not exist, Oct 2020); now ~$4.99/mo or $49.99/yr (advanced stats, custom charts, unlimited recommendations, roadmap vote); only ~10.5k subscribers of 2.3M (~0.4%), blamed partly on a 30-day trial that removed urgency; Plus is being refocused on advanced stats because the grab-bag "lacked product-market fit". Publisher-paid giveaways ("to users it's not really ads") may out-earn Plus. Profitable since 2022; team of ~3 by choice.

### 1.4 Untappd
Greg Avola and Tim Mather met on Twitter, lived on opposite coasts, built Untappd nights and weekends for five years on a $1,000 initial outlay, and joined Next Glass in 2015. ~2.8-3M registered users at the time of the Braze case study; ~9M users and 900M+ check-ins by 2021; New Year's Eve peak of 1,100 check-ins per minute. Retention mechanics: badges that require repeated check-ins (the "Radler" badge needs five Radler-style beers), with the community voting every two months on the next wave; a three-feed design - Friends, Global, Nearby - so a brand-new user with no friends still sees a living app ("even if a new user didn't have any friends on the app"); Nearby/Trending/Top Rated as reasons to open; and breweries claiming profiles and putting "check in on Untappd" signage in taprooms, an offline-to-online loop. The founders explicitly designed against the industry baseline that half of users are gone a week after install.

### 1.5 Discogs
Started in 2000 by Kevin Lewandowski (an Intel programmer) on a computer in his closet, electronic music only. The Wantlist and Collection features arrived in 2002 and are still the two most-used features. The Marketplace (2005) exists because users with collections and wantlists "asked for a sell list"; its revenue paid for the first employee. 602k+ contributors, 17M+ releases; collections cataloged 920M+ items at end-2025, the largest single-year increase in 25 years; marketplace sales +30% in 2020. Lesson: the collection/wantlist pair is a complete single-player loop; the network (marketplace, contributor community) was layered on once the catalog had value, and community ownership of the database is what makes it durable.

### 1.6 Last.fm
UK, 2002; merged with Audioscrobbler 2003-2005. In the mid/late 2000s it combined free radio, an automatic log of everything you played, and a heavy social layer: friends, groups, shoutboxes, messaging, concert lists, a taste-compatibility meter, and profile widgets you could embed on MySpace or Facebook. Channel 4 based a chart show on scrobbles; Portishead premiered *Third* on Last.fm a week early (2008). Why scrobbling stuck: zero-effort capture, and the log became identity ("top artists" charts embedded elsewhere). Why it faded: the 2009 TechCrunch/RIAA U2-leak accusation cost trust; radio was paywalled (~2010) and killed (2014); the 15 April 2015 redesign drew "universally negative" forum reactions - groups removed, About Me capped at 200 characters with no images, friend comparisons missing; one user: "The redesign killed the social aspect of last.fm." It survives as a passive utility with almost no social layer, and when the passive capture breaks (Spotify's 2026 token change) the utility breaks. Lesson: strip the social layer from a tracker and the tracker survives but the community does not.

### 1.7 Airbuds
Gilles Poupardin and Gawen Arab (ex-Zenly) launched Airbuds in October 2022 after a prior app, Capuccino. By September 2025: 15M+ downloads, 5M MAU, 1.5M DAU (a 30% DAU/MAU ratio), 96% positive ratings; $10M raised (a16z, SV Angel, Seven Seven Six). Users are mostly US high-school and college students. The mechanic: connect Spotify/Apple Music/SoundCloud/etc. and every play is shared automatically - "it's effortless... every time you're going to listen to something on Spotify, it's going to be shared on Airbuds in real time." A home-screen widget shows what friends are playing without opening the app; reactions are emoji, stickers or selfies; a Weekly Recap arrives every Sunday - the peer pitch student ambassadors use is "Spotify Wrapped, but every week." Feature gating drives invites: you can only see beyond your top-3 artists in the recap after inviting friends. A customizable "Space" profile (favorite artists, songs, lyrics, photos, text) is the identity surface; ~30% of users go beyond passively watching friends' streams. This is Last.fm's scrobble rebuilt for the lock screen and the group chat, with the recap doing the job notifications used to.

### 1.8 Beli
Judy Thelen and Eliot Frost (HBS, ex-McKinsey) turned a shared Google Map of NYC restaurants into an app: beta during COVID on Harvard grants, App Store June 2021, $5.3M Series A (Goodwater, Nov 2023), $12M total (FirstMark, Jun 2025). Reviews: 13M (2023) -> 30M (2024) -> 60-75M cumulative by Sep 2025 across 30k cities (Yelp logged 22M and 21M in the same two years). "80 percent of users today are under age 35 and join the app through referrals." The mechanic: no star ratings - each new restaurant is placed by pairwise comparison against ones you have already ranked ("higher than X? lower than Y?") and the app derives a score out of ten, which the founders say "leads to more honest and personalized feedback." You only see friends' reviews, never strangers'; each restaurant shows two numbers - a predicted score for you (from taste similarity) and your friends' score. A map of visited and want-to-try places; "Want to Try" bookmarking straight from Instagram and TikTok posts; city and global leaderboards, weekly ranking streaks, referral counts, college competitions across 187 universities with linked student IDs (UChicago, NYU, Brown), OpenTable and Hinge integrations. No DMs. Monetization is an invite-only NYC Supper Club (subscription plus events). Critiques: streak pressure ("prioritizing streaks over genuine dining"), herd mentality toward hyped restaurants, and the comparison problem ("How do I compare the taco stand on the side of the road that I love with a three-Michelin-star restaurant?").

### 1.9 Trakt
Sean Rudford and Justin Nemeth shipped Trakt as a scrobbling plugin for XBMC (Kodi) in fall 2010. A March 2011 Lifehacker feature took it from 1,200 to 3,000 users overnight; growth then came from the ecosystem - Kodi, Plex, Emby, Infuse and dozens of third-party client apps built on the API - to ~200k users (2012) and 500k+ (May 2014). VIP includes ad-free, advanced stats and filters, collaborative lists, 100 personal notes, and daily/real-time auto-sync of watched history from Netflix, Prime, Hulu, Apple TV+, Plex and Kodi. Per-episode "shouts" and reviews exist but the community is small and utilitarian. In 2025 Trakt tightened free-tier limits (Feb) and forced legacy $15-30/yr VIP plans to a standard $60/yr (June), reneging on a promise to honor legacy pricing; users complained of "costs doubling without any new features." Lesson: API-first trackers grow through other people's apps and monetize sync convenience, but a utility-only tracker has thin pricing power, so price hikes read as betrayal.

### 1.10 TV Time (died July 2026)
TVShow Time, a small Paris startup (founder Antonio Pinto), was acquired in December 2016 by Whipclip, a TV-clip app that had failed on rights; the combined company became TV Time, then Whip Media (bought Mediamorph 2019; raised $50M Jan 2020). ~1M DAU and 12M registered (Oct 2019); 20M registered (late 2021); 26.4M lifetime installs and still ~29k downloads a month in mid-2026. Half of users contributed data (follow, comment, like, review). The core ritual was per-episode: mark watched, then read the episode's comment thread - Pinto: "Reading the community reactions after each episode became a ritual for me, and for many others." The consumer app was never the business; TVLytics sold sentiment, ratings predictions and audience data to studios and agencies. After Blue Torch Capital took over Whip Media in early 2025 the company pivoted to an enterprise AI product (Helix) and shut TV Time on 15 July 2026, citing "unsustainable operating costs and insufficient demand for a paid model." Pinto: the "premium subscription plan only covered about 10% of those expenses due to the size of its community." He immediately built Bingers, engineered to "keep its server costs low" and stay fast at marking episodes during peak, importing TV Time's GDPR export so users keep their history and comments. Lessons: (1) episode-granular tracking is the most expensive kind per user; (2) 25M users plus a data-licensing model still dies if the consumer product is not self-funding; (3) an orphaned community migrates to whoever can import its history.

### 1.11 Douban
Founded March 2005 by Yang Bo (ex-IBM) to find "like-minded people through the things they like." The product chain grew books -> films -> music -> city events -> Groups. The mechanic that went mainstream in China is a two-verb journal - "想看" (want to watch) and "看过" (watched) - with a five-star rating; the aggregate score became a box-office force (a Fudan researcher in 2015: a higher opening-weekend Douban score was worth ~350k yuan extra), and "rating participants expanded from movie fans to the general public." 100M+ monthly users (2012); 196M registered and ~100M monthly active film users (Q2 2019). Decline: the company split its single app into 13 separate mobile apps in the 2010s (ChinaTalk: the slide "traces back to one specific decision"); censorship deleted popular Groups and blocked newsfeed features; fandom "rating-bombing" under industry pressure and an influx of gossip-oriented users polluted the ratings the core users trusted; Douban stopped publishing growth numbers. A devoted user: strangers there "know what you are thinking even better than folks on WeChat."

### 1.12 MyAnimeList and AniList
MAL is the largest anime community (forums, "recognizable scores in wider anime culture", deepest catalog for older titles); AniList is the better tracker (UI, flexible scoring, independent, active development). Many serious fans run both: AniList as the daily list, MAL as the reference. Completion culture: lists have Watching / Completed / On Hold / Dropped / Plan to Watch states plus "days watched" totals and mean score, and the list is the identity - screenshotted, embedded in forum signatures, compared. Completionists describe tracking as "a blessing and a curse... the deep satisfaction of finishing every episode... and knowing a series inside out"; the practical driver is memory ("realize six months later that they watched it before and forgot") and the pull of "seeing everything at once."

Sources: https://www.achriom.com/blog/anilist-vs-myanimelist/ ; https://kosonga.com/best-myanimelist-alternatives/ ; https://www.ardentworkshop.com/blog/anime-manga-tracking-completionists/

### 1.13 Backloggd and Serializd
Backloggd ("Letterboxd for games") is a solo project by "JackBros": started Aug 2018, 1.0 Aug 2019, went full-time in January 2025 funded by Patreon - 350k+ registered users, ~1,300 patrons (~0.4%), with a stated goal of closing the sustainability gap by 2026. No Android app; slow feature velocity is the main user complaint. Serializd (TV) is a free solo project by Humad Syed positioned as "to TV what Letterboxd is to movies." Lesson: a Letterboxd-shaped product in an adjacent vertical can reach hundreds of thousands of users on word of mouth from the Letterboxd crowd, and ~0.4% patron conversion funds one person, not a team.

Sources: https://www.resetera.com/threads/the-creator-of-backloggd-letterboxd-for-games-is-moving-it-from-a-side-project-in-his-spare-time-to-his-full-time-job-thanks-to-patreon-support.1073388/ ; https://backloggd.medium.com/a-new-year-a-new-beginning-8434a5737bcd ; https://backloggd.com/about/ ; https://www.serializd.com/ ; https://www.twoaveragegamers.com/backloggd-review/

### 1.14 Duolingo
Jorge Mazal (ex-CPO) in Lenny's Newsletter: their growth model showed Current User Retention Rate had "5 times the impact" of the next lever, so they spent four years on it - CURR +21%, equivalent to a "reduction in the daily churn of our best users by over 40%"; DAU 4.5x in four years; the share of DAU with a 7+ day streak nearly tripled to more than half. Users who reach a 10-day streak have "substantially" lower dropout risk. What worked: leagues matched by engagement level rather than friendship (learning time +17%, highly-engaged users tripled); streak-saver late-night pushes; streak freeze, calendar views, milestone animations; treating push as a channel to protect - "dozens of small- and medium-size wins" on timing, copy and localization, never volume (they cite Groupon "destroying their channel" with aggressive email). What failed: a Gardenscapes-style moves counter ("completely neutral. No change to our retention"); an Uber-style referral program (+3% new users, because the best users already paid). Their rule for copying: ask "Why is this feature working in that product?" and "What adaptations are necessary?" Duolingo's own A/B tests: a 7-day streak wager lifted D7 retention +14%; a weekend amulet made users 4% more likely to return a week later and 5% less likely to lose the streak, and "learners who binge... were much more likely to abandon the app" - "by giving learners the option to take a break, they're actually more likely to do more in the long run." Costs: the "These reminders don't seem to be working. We'll stop sending them for now." notification exists because nagging a month-lapsed user is pointless; the passive-aggressive owl became a "Duolingo needs to chill" backlash; third-party data shows higher churn after a streak breaks (users abandon rather than restart), satisfaction falling after ~day 90 from "unhealthy pressure", and streak-freeze access keeping streaks 4.5x longer by day 21; an academic case study documents users optimizing XP instead of learning.

Sources: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth ; https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals ; https://debugger.medium.com/duolingo-needs-to-chill-8f1832745ca0 ; https://trophy.so/blog/what-happens-when-users-lose-streaks ; https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2026/02/designing-streak-system-ux-psychology/ ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.16175

### 1.15 BeReal
August 2022: 73.5M MAU and #1 free app. The mechanic - one random daily push, two minutes to post a front-and-back photo, retakes and late posts flagged - is a textbook forced ritual. By October 2022 only 9% of Android installs opened daily; MAU fell by more than half in two years. Diagnoses: the notification "started to feel more like a chore and less like a reward"; users began "performing to be someone you are not"; no meaningful feature iteration for months ("novelty without iteration"); friends-of-friends and mentions arrived too late. Voodoo bought it for $537M (June 2024), rebuilt it ("BeReal 2.0", video, chat), launched US ads in April 2025 claiming 40M MAU (third-party trackers said ~16M), 85% Gen Z, and reached ~$30M revenue by end-2025. Lesson: an app-initiated ritual with no accumulating artifact decays; rituals must be user-initiated (I watched something) and must leave something behind that grows.

### 1.16 Foursquare / Swarm

## 2. Cold start: how they got the first 1,000 engaged users

| Product | First 1,000 | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Letterboxd | Conference demo (Brooklyn Beta, Oct 2011) -> invite-only beta (Apr 2012-Feb 2013); 17k testers in 6 months | Seed a specific tribe (web designers who love film), scarcity via invites, let them fill the site before the public sees it |
| StoryGraph | 3 months of interviews -> landing page -> weekly email to 100 -> 100 users (Jan 2020) -> 1,000 (Jun 2020) | Hand-recruited avid readers, Goodreads CSV import, then a viral moment she did not plan |
| Trakt | Plugin for an existing enthusiast community (XBMC) -> Lifehacker feature (1,200 -> 3,000 overnight) | Attach to a community that already has the behavior |
| Pinterest | Founder emailed the first 5,000, meetups at boutiques, invites to people with projects | Recruit power users by hand, don't change the product |
| Untappd | Craft-beer Twitter + Foursquare check-in culture; Global/Nearby feeds so no one sees an empty room | Borrow an existing verb (check-in) and an existing tribe |
| Beli | Founders' friends at HBS -> NYC -> campuses with school leaderboards; 80% join by referral | Friends-only visibility makes each invite necessary, not optional |
| Airbuds | US high schools/colleges; student ambassadors; invite-gated recap features | Passive capture means day one has content; gating makes invites the price of features |
| Wordle | Family -> NZ friends -> emoji grid | The share artifact is the growth loop |

**Doctrine.** Chris Dixon (2015): "come for the tool, stay for the network" - Delicious (bookmark cloud -> tag discovery), Instagram (filters + cross-posting -> its own network). Marc Bodnick's 2016 rebuttal: the theory is "overstated"; Instagram's sharing and following were "critical from the beginning" and "building a social product is almost always more challenging than building a great single-player tool" - so design the social object on day one even if the network arrives later. Andrew Chen: find the atomic network, "probably smaller and more specific than you think... on the order of hundreds of people, at a specific moment in time" (Zoom = 2, Slack = 3, Airbnb = hundreds of listings in one city). Nikita Bier: show core value "within the first three seconds"; contact sync built tbh and Gas; "the number of invitations sent per user drops 20% for every additional year of age from 13 to 18"; a durable social product is "a black swan event that happens maybe once a decade"; getting "7 adult friends to install an app on a reproducible basis is non-trivial."

**Import as growth lever.** StoryGraph called the Goodreads import "critical onboarding"; Letterboxd shipped IMDb/CSV import as a Pro feature at launch; Bingers launched by importing TV Time's GDPR export; Trakt's whole model is importing from players. Every tracker death (TV Time) is a cohort looking for somewhere to import into.

## 3. What makes people share a profile or list publicly

1. **A ranked, finite identity slot.** Four posters (Letterboxd), eight friends (MySpace), a top-3 artists card (Airbuds), a "days watched" number (MAL). Finite slots force choice, and choice is a statement. It is now a first-date question and a Hinge prompt; a Gen Z "personality test." The cost is performance (Izzy Scott: choosing films for "the 'aesthetic' you want to fit into online"), which is tolerable because the object is a film, not a friend.
2. **A periodic recap about me.** Wrapped, Year in Sport, Year in Review, Airbuds' Sunday recap, Goodreads' challenge progress. Shared because it is flattering, comparable and time-boxed.
3. **A spoiler-free, comparable artifact.** Wordle's grid; a Beli score; a Strava map. The share must not require the viewer to have the app to understand it, and must invite "what's yours?"
4. **A decorated home.** MySpace HTML profiles, Airbuds "Space", Letterboxd Patron custom posters/backdrops, Discogs collection pages. People pay for decoration (Letterboxd Patron) and return to maintain it.
5. **A list that helps someone else.** Letterboxd lists (+88% in 2025), Pinterest boards, Discogs wantlists. Lists are the most shareable object because they are useful to the recipient, not just expressive of the sender.

Sources: sections 1.1, 1.7, 1.12, 1.21, 1.22 above.

## 4. Small-group social (2-6 people)

- **The recurring moment exists and is measurable.** Nielsen 2023: 11 minutes on average deciding what to stream (up from 7 two years earlier); couples report up to 30 minutes. Netflix's own survey: 40% of Netflix-using couples want to watch different things, 65% negotiate, 35% trade shows, and couples bicker more than cable viewers; sharing a Netflix password is "a big relationship step." "Shared Watchlist: Couple match" apps (swipe-to-match on titles) are an App Store category; Moviebase and others publish couple-tracking guides.
- **Friends-only visibility is a retention feature, not a limitation.** Beli shows only friends' reviews and computes a friends' score next to your predicted score; 80% of users join by referral because the app is empty without friends. StoryGraph's buddy reads and book clubs (voting, agendas, meeting journals) are its most-requested social features. Strava's 12-month retention lift for group-activity loggers is the cleanest number on group accountability in this research.
- **The group chat is where the reaction lives.** TV Time's per-episode thread was the ritual; Discord servers run per-episode channels; "six people in one chaotic thread" is the modal watching community. A tracker should produce the artifact that gets pasted into the chat (a card: "we watched X, she gave it 4, I gave it 2") rather than trying to host the chat.
- **Partner-based accountability** works when the artifact is shared (a joint watchlist that shrinks, a challenge both pledged to) and fails when it is competitive (leaderboards among couples) - Beli's college leaderboards work for a campus, not for a couple.

**Pattern.** People pay for (a) statistics and recaps about themselves, (b) decorating their profile, (c) convenience of automatic capture/sync, (d) supporting a small team they like. Realistic conversion for a small tracker is 0.4-4%; plan infrastructure so 1% paying covers 100% of costs, which is exactly what TV Time could not do.

## 7. Anti-patterns and failure modes for a small team in 2026, with mitigations

1. **Empty room.** A new user with no friends sees nothing. Mitigation: Untappd's three feeds (Friends / Global / Nearby); Letterboxd's "popular this week" and community ratings as the default surface; passive capture (Airbuds) so day one has content; import so the diary is full before the first friend arrives.
2. **Feed of verbs, not voices.** "X rated Y 3 stars" x 40 is data, not a reason to open the app. Letterboxd's feed is reviews and lists (text with a person behind it); Goodreads' feed is status updates. Mitigation: make the unit of the feed a sentence, a list or a recap card, and let a friend's rating appear next to yours on the film page rather than as a stream item.
3. **Over-gamification.** Beli's weekly streaks produce "prioritizing streaks over genuine dining"; Duolingo's satisfaction drops after ~day 90; users optimize XP. Mitigation: annual pledges (Goodreads challenge) and milestone celebrations instead of daily streaks; if streaks exist, freeze/repair by default and never notify about a break.
4. **Forced ritual.** BeReal's decay. Mitigation: only user-initiated logging; the app's job is to make the moment after watching frictionless, not to create the moment.
5. **Harassment and review bombing.** Goodreads (Corrain, extortion), Douban (fandom rating-bombing), and now AI-written reviews from fake accounts. Mitigation: StoryGraph's subtraction (no DMs, no comments on reviews), Beli's friends-only visibility, Letterboxd's "hide rather than delete" plus a two-line policy; rate limits on new accounts; aggregate ratings weighted toward established accounts.
6. **Bot and AI content.** Cheap to generate, poisons aggregates and trust. Mitigation: value the friend graph over the crowd (only friends' ratings shown by default), require a logged watch to review, and keep human moderation load bounded by not having public comment threads at all in v1.
7. **Moderation load.** Letterboxd has 40+ staff; StoryGraph runs ~3 people by refusing to host conversations. Mitigation: pick the StoryGraph side until there is budget.
8. **Metadata dependency (TMDB).** Free only for non-commercial use with attribution; commercial license $149/month under $1M revenue; ~40 req/s per IP; no caching beyond six months; episode data for TV is the weakest part (Letterboxd's TV rollout has slipped for three years partly for this reason). Mitigation: cache aggressively within terms, mirror IDs to Wikidata/TheTVDB/IMDb, own your own edition/episode overrides, and budget the commercial license from day one.
9. **Infrastructure cost per user vs 1% conversion.** TV Time's premium covered 10% of costs; Bingers is being rebuilt around cheap writes. Mitigation: design the episode-watched write path to be nearly free; avoid per-user fan-out feeds; precompute recaps.
10. **Removing the social layer to cut costs.** Last.fm 2015: "killed the social aspect." Douban splitting into 13 apps. Mitigation: never fragment the product; if you must cut, cut broadcast features, not friend features.
11. **Owner pivot risk.** TV Time (AI pivot), Goodreads (Amazon neglect), Foursquare (B2B). Mitigation: be self-funding by users; publish an export.

## 8. Cross-cutting mechanics that reliably retained (ranked)

1. **An accumulating personal artifact that is public by default.** The diary, collection, wantlist, map, streak calendar. Evidence: Discogs collections at 920M items and still growing after 25 years; Letterboxd logging 898.5M films in 2025; Pinterest's boards ("collections they don't want to lose"); Goodreads' challenge as a year-long progress bar. Products that lacked one (Foursquare check-ins, BeReal photos) decayed.
2. **Passive or two-tap capture.** Strava GPS, Last.fm scrobbles, Airbuds widget (30% DAU/MAU), Trakt/Plex sync; where passive is impossible, Douban's "want to watch / watched" two-verb log. Effort is the first churn driver.
3. **A finite identity slot on the profile.** Four Favorites (10M+ TikTok views; a dating-app question), MySpace Top 8, Airbuds Space. Cheap to maintain, expensive to abandon, and it markets the product.
4. **A periodic recap about the user.** Wrapped (+21% downloads), Year in Sport, Year in Review (4M+ views), Airbuds weekly recap ("Wrapped, but every week"), Goodreads challenge. The only notification users ask for.
5. **A small, real friend graph with friends-first visibility.** Beli (friends-only reviews, 80% referral joins), Strava clubs and group activities (12-month retention lift), StoryGraph buddy reads, TV Time's episode threads. Not the follower count.
6. **Community-owned data.** Discogs contributors, TMDB itself, Untappd badge votes. Ownership creates maintenance behavior.
7. **Utility alerts tied to the artifact.** New episode aired (TV Time/Hobi), watchlist title now streaming (JustWatch 60M MAU, Letterboxd Pro), author's new book (Goodreads). Wanted because the user created the trigger.
8. **Forgiving streaks and pledges.** Duolingo: 10-day streak lowers dropout; freeze keeps streaks 4.5x longer; weekend amulet +4% return; annual pledges (Goodreads) work without daily pressure.
9. **Civility by design.** StoryGraph's subtraction; Letterboxd's "be cool" and hide-not-delete; Beli's no-DMs. Safety is a retention feature because harassment is a churn event.
10. **Import and export.** Every migration wave (Goodreads -> StoryGraph, TV Time -> Bingers) was won by whoever could ingest the diary.

## 9. Mechanics that reliably corroded (ranked)

1. **App-initiated daily rituals** (BeReal: 73.5M MAU to 9% daily-open in two months).
2. **Guilt-based notification copy and volume** (Duolingo backlash; Groupon's dead channel; post-streak-break churn).
3. **Paywalling previously free social features or repricing without value** (Strava 2020 backlash; Trakt 2025 "300% hike").
4. **Stripping social features in a redesign** (Last.fm 2015 "killed the social aspect"; Douban's 13-app split).
5. **Public comment threads and DMs on reviews without moderation budget** (Goodreads review bombing, extortion, sock puppets).
6. **Crowd ratings over friend ratings** once the crowd is polluted (Douban rating-bombing; Goodreads; AI reviews); the Letterboxd critique that strangers' stars override a partner's recommendation.
7. **Competitive leaderboards among people who know each other** (MySpace Top 8 "psychological warfare"; Beli streak pressure) - competition works among strangers matched by engagement (Duolingo leagues), not among friends.
8. **Feature stagnation after a spike** (BeReal "novelty without iteration"; Goodreads post-2013).
9. **Broadcast-to-everyone verbs** (Foursquare check-ins; only 5% of sessions used social and discovery together).
10. **Consumer product subsidized by a non-consumer business** (TV Time's data business; Whip Media's pivot killed 25M users' diaries).

## 10. Cold-start playbook for a movie+TV journal in 2026 (sequenced)

**Phase 0 - before code ships (weeks 0-4).** Interview 20-30 people who already keep a log somewhere (Letterboxd, Notes app, a group chat, a shared Google Sheet with a partner), StoryGraph-style; recruit by cold-DMing people who post Letterboxd screenshots and "what should we watch" polls. Ship a landing page with one sentence and a weekly email to the first 100 that reads like a stand-up. Decide the social object now (Bodnick), even if the network comes later: the object is a shared watchlist between 2-6 people plus a personal diary.

**Phase 1 - single-player that is full on day one (weeks 4-10).** Import first: Letterboxd CSV, Trakt, IMDb, TV Time GDPR export, JustWatch watchlist; the diary must not be empty. Two-verb logging (want / watched) in two taps from a share sheet, with an optional half-sentence. TV: mark-episode-watched must be the cheapest write in the system (Bingers' lesson). Give every profile a finite identity slot (four favorites, or "four for right now" that rotates) and a stats page from day one - stats are what people pay for later. Budget the TMDB commercial license and cache within terms; own episode overrides.

**Phase 2 - atomic network = one household or one friend group (weeks 8-16).** The invite is not "join my network" but "here is our shared watchlist" - a link sent by text (Partiful's model). Friends-only visibility by default (Beli). The recurring moment is "what should we watch tonight": a shortlist computed from the intersection of two watchlists plus availability on the services both people have; time saved is the measurable value (11-30 minutes). When both log the same title, generate a comparison card ("you 4, her 2") that pastes into iMessage/WhatsApp/Discord - the tracker produces the artifact the group chat consumes.

**Phase 3 - seed a tribe, not a demographic (weeks 12-24).** Invite-only for a defined community (Letterboxd used web designers; use one film-Discord, one university film society, one BookTok-adjacent creator's audience, one city's rep cinema). Nikita Bier's arithmetic says invites per user fall 20% per year of age from 13 to 18, so favor campuses and film-club Discords if the product is for young people, or couples/households if it is not - and know which one it is. Untappd's rule: no empty rooms - show "popular this week among people like you" and a global feed until a user has three friends (Slack's stable number).

**Phase 4 - the loops (month 4 onward).** (a) Monthly and annual recap cards with share images, plus a "pledge" (Goodreads challenge) set in January and in September. (b) Utility alerts only from user-created triggers: watchlist title now streaming on your services; new episode of a show you are mid-way through; a friend logged a title on your watchlist (framed as "X just watched Y - ask them" not as feed noise). (c) Lists that help others. (d) A "be cool" policy, hide-not-delete moderation, no DMs and no comments on reviews in v1; conversation happens in the group chat you generate cards for.

**Phase 5 - monetize what people already pay for (month 6+).** Pro at $19-49/yr: stats, custom posters/backdrops, availability alerts, extra import/export, supporter badge; pre-sell it StoryGraph-style. Never paywall the friend graph or the diary. Assume 1-4% conversion and make infra fit inside it; publish a data export from day one as a trust signal.

**Phase 6 - the identity format as marketing (month 6+).** Turn the profile slot into a video format (Four Favorites, "what are you two watching?") with local filmmakers, film-society presidents and creators; every episode of the format is an ad for having a profile.

## 11. Notification and re-engagement doctrine

1. **Only send notifications the user caused.** A watchlist title became available; an episode of a show they are mid-way through aired; a friend logged a title on their watchlist; someone replied to them. Never "we miss you," never "keep your streak," never "you haven't logged in 7 days."
2. **Once per day per category, batched, with a quiet window.** Letterboxd caps availability alerts at once daily; Strava lets each club be set to announcements-only; Duolingo protects the channel with timing and copy, not volume.
3. **Interaction subscribes, silence unsubscribes.** Commenting or reacting subscribes you to that thread (Letterboxd, Strava); a month of ignored pushes auto-stops them with a note (Duolingo). Ship the bell-to-mute on every thread.
4. **Make the recap the notification.** A Sunday or monthly recap ("you two watched 6 things; she rated higher 5 times") and a yearly card - opt-in, shareable, about the user. This replaces most re-engagement email.
5. **Release-day and on-this-day are the only calendar pushes.** "Season 3 of X starts tonight" and "one year ago you two watched Y" (Timehop/Facebook memories: 90M visited in test). Both come from the diary, not from the app's needs.
6. **Never notify about a broken streak.** Users churn harder after the break than before it; if streaks exist, freeze silently and celebrate milestones only.
7. **Suppress low-quality events.** Strava's downhill-KOM rule: if the event would embarrass the app or the user, do not send it. Losing a rating comparison, being unfollowed, a stranger's like - none of these earn a push.
8. **Digest email weekly, opt-in, with the friend group's activity and the intersection watchlist at the top.** The email's job is to answer "what should we watch this week", not to report activity.
9. **Widgets and share sheets beat pushes.** Airbuds' home-screen widget and a lock-screen "what's on our list tonight" widget deliver presence without interrupting; the share-sheet is the logging entry point.
10. **Measure CURR, not DAU.** Duolingo found current-user retention had 5x the leverage of any other lever; for a journal, the metric is "logged something in the last 30 days" and "opened a shared list this week."
